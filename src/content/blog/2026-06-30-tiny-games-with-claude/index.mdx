My latest obsession with AI is spinning up stupid little games for my family and getting them to play them. The idea of creating games has always appealed to me, but the undertaking of making one always intimidated me and felt like too big of a commitment, especially now that I'm a parent with a toddler. Claude, my AI tool of choice, totally removed this barrier and made it easy to play around with game design.

Are these games good? Obviously not. Did they bring some joy to the family members I shared them with? Absolutely. 

Until now, I hadn't shared these with anyone outside my family members or friends, so it does feel a bit like by making them public I'm contributing to the slop heap. These games definitely meet that criteria. I didn't spend more than a couple days on any of them. At the same time they do have a similar charm to me that old flash games did on the early internet, which definitely took a lot more work to make but had the same rudimentary appearance. I wanted to write about it, not only because they were fun to make, but also because it feels like it could be the start of a new creative pursuit.

Maybe I'm kidding myself. All of the same hardships that scared me away from game design are still there. AI just makes it easier to imagine what a game I made could look like. I suppose right now I'm exploring that fantasy. We'll see if anything comes from it.

## Cookie monster touch game for my son

The first game I made with Claude was for my toddler. I had the idea of creating something very simple he could interact with on my phone. He was going through a Sesame Street phase, so that idea took the shape of a Cookie Monster avatar chasing down and eating cookies that appeared on the screen.

While I believed that Claude could help me create this game, I expected it would still take some work, maybe a week. What ended up happening was I was able to get a working game in the couple hours my son was taking his mid-day nap.

For my first prompt I explained the concept to Claude (in the regular chat interface) and asked it to help me think through it. It asked some follow up questions about my son's age range, how many cookies on the screen at once, and whether cookie monster should run to each cookie or teleport. I gave it my answers, and it produced the first iteration.

[gif of first version]

I was impressed that I already felt close to the finish line of what I had been imagining. I told the AI to go from a one-dimensional movement plane to two and use a simple background. That did what I wanted, and the only other addition was I downloaded a sound bite of the classic "OM NOM NOM" to play each time the avatar ate a cookie. I had my finished game!

I've made a couple games for my son since then, but this is the only one that he's asked to play again. Creating something that he enjoyed and getting to see him interact with the game in the way I intended sparked something inside me that started me down this AI game dev rabbit hole.

## Protecting birdfeeders from squirrels game for my wife

The second game I wanted to talk about was one I made for my wife, inspired by her frustration with squirrels stealing birdseed from her birdfeeders in the backyard. I thought making a game out of it could help her get out some of that frustration and allow her to laugh about it.

Same as with the Cookie Monster game, I explained the concept to Claude, but this time I skipped the thinking through part and went straight to asking it to create the game. Perhaps that was a mistake. I do think that with AI because it makes things quicker and easier, if you're not careful you'll slip into a mindset of wanting to rush through everything.

Nevertheless, the prototype it came up with was solid. I added some elements like baffles being a line of defense, the rate of squirrels increasing as game goes on, and the appearance of giant squirrels at a certain point. 

At this point I shared it with my wife. She liked it and definitely took out some of her frustration as intended, but it turned out to be way too easy.

For this first version, to dispatch the squirrels all you had to do was tap them. It was easy for my wife to keep pace doing this with the rate at which they appeared. I told the game it was too easy, so it increased the squirrel rate, made them do more damage to the birdfeeders, and added "sneaky squirrels" that moved faster. I played that version but still found it too easy.

That's when I came up with the idea to change that mechanic. I figured that it would be hard to keep up with the squirrels on different parts of the screen if you had to drag your thumb rather than being able to pick it up and tap. Along these lines, I told Claude to have the player use a water hose to attack the squirrels and to make it possible to deal friendly fire to the birds that came to eat on the birdfeeders.

It spun up a new version with the hose, but the area of effect didn't seem to be quite right. I decided to move off the chat interface and create a GitHub repo so I could work on this with Claude Code. It was able to get the hose working right but now the game was too difficult.

To mitigate, I decided to create multiple levels with increasing difficulty. I let my wife play again and she enjoyed it, but as the player she instinctually wanted to be able to refill the bird feeders in some way. I liked that idea and got Claude to implement, where by holding your thumb on the birdfeeder it would refill.

By this time, my wife was a little tired of being my guinea pig, and it was becoming more about me wanting to improve the game then the original intention of bringing her joy, so I stopped working on it there, but this experience was my first foray into thinking about game mechanics and how changing them could alter difficulty.
