My latest obsession with AI is spinning up stupid little video games for my family and getting them to play them. I've always imagined creating games like this, but all of the programming required always intimidated me, especially now that my free time is limited as a parent with a toddler. But Claude, my AI tool of choice, has largely removed that barrier and made it easy to play around with game design.

Are these games good? Definitely not. Did they bring some joy to the family members I shared them with? Totally. 

Until now, I hadn't shared these with anyone outside my family members or friends, so it does feel a bit like I'm contributing to the slop heap by making them public. These games certainly meet that criteria. I didn't spend more than a couple days on any of them. 

At the same time, they do have a similar charm as old flash games did on the early internet. And they were fun to make, so much so it almost feels like the opening of a whole new filed of creative pursuit for me. 

Maybe I'm kidding myself. All of the same hardships that scared me away from game design are still there in terms of actually making something a lot of people could play, that would be reliable and polished. AI just makes it easier to prototype. For now that's good enough.

## Cookie Monster game for my son

The first game I made with Claude was for my toddler. I had the idea of creating something very simple he could interact with on my phone. He was going through a Sesame Street phase, so that idea took the shape of a Cookie Monster sprite chasing down and eating cookies that appeared on the screen.

While I believed that Claude could help me create this game, I expected it would still take some work, maybe a week. What ended up happening was I was able to get a working game in a couple hours, as my son was taking his mid-day nap.

For my first prompt I explained the concept to Claude (in the regular chat interface) and asked it to help me think through it. The model asked some follow up questions about my son's age range, how many cookies should be on the screen at once, and whether Cookie Monster should run to each cookie or teleport. I gave it my answers, and it produced the first iteration.

[gif of first version]

I was impressed that I already felt close to the finish line of what I had been envisioning. I told the AI to go from a one-dimensional movement plane to two and use a simple background. That did what I wanted, and the only other addition was  a sound bite of the classic "OM NOM NOM" to play each time the sprite ate a cookie. I had my finished my crappy game!

I've made a couple games for my son since then, but this is the only one that he's asked to play again. Creating something that he enjoyed and getting to see him interact with the game in the way I intended sparked something inside me that started me down this AI game dev rabbit hole.

## Protecting birdfeeders from squirrels game for my wife

The second game I'd like to highlight was one I made for my wife, inspired by her frustration with squirrels stealing birdseed from our birdfeeders in the backyard. I thought making a game out of it could help alleviate some of that exasperation and get her to laugh about it.

Same as with the Cookie Monster game, I explained the concept to Claude, but this time I skipped the thinking through part and went straight to asking it to create the game. Perhaps that was a mistake. I do think that with AI because it makes things quicker and easier, if you're not careful you'll slip into a mindset of wanting to rush through everything.

Nevertheless, the prototype it came up with was solid. I added some elements like baffles being a line of defense, the rate of squirrels increasing as game goes on, and the appearance of giant squirrels at a certain point. 

At this point I shared it with my wife. She liked it and definitely enjoyed decimating the squirrels (maybe too much??), but the game itself turned out to be way too easy.

For this first version, to dispatch the squirrels all you had to do was tap them. It was easy for my wife to keep pace doing this with the rate at which they appeared. I told Claude the game was too easy, so it increased the squirrel rate, made them do more damage to the birdfeeders, and added "sneaky squirrels" that moved faster. I played that version but still found it too easy.

That's when I came up with the idea to change the mechanic for dispatching squirrels. I figured that it would be hard to keep up with the squirrels on different parts of the screen if you had to drag your thumb rather than being able to pick it up and tap. Along these lines, I told Claude to have the player use a water hose to attack the squirrels and to make it possible to deal friendly fire to the birds that came to eat on the birdfeeders.

It spun up a new version with the hose, but the area of effect didn't seem to be quite right. I decided to move off the chat interface and create a GitHub repo so I could work on this with Claude Code. It was able to get the hose working right but now the game was too difficult.

To mitigate, I decided to create multiple levels with increasing difficulty. I let my wife play again and she had fun, but as the player she instinctually wanted to be able to refill the bird feeders in some way. I liked that idea and got Claude to implement, where by holding your thumb on the birdfeeder it would refill.

By this time, my wife was a little tired of being my game design guinea pig, and it was becoming more about me wanting to improve the game then the original intention of bringing her joy, so I stopped working on it. Even if it was short-lived, this experience was educational as my first foray into thinking about game mechanics and how changing them could alter difficulty.

## Feeding cats game for my mom

Last game I'll mention is one I made for my mom for her birthday. Like any lovable grandma, she has a habit of bringing out food for the stray cats of her neighborhood, so of course I had to make a game about it.

This time I created a GitHub repo from the get go and did the first prompt in Claude Code. I gave the AI the reference of Overcooked in terms of the vibe I was going for and the scramble to get food out for multiple customers quickly. My family also has a dark sense of humor, so I also instructed the model to make the cats explode if they didn't get their food after their timer ran out.

The challenge for this game was the art. Claude's first attempt at creating sprites for my mom and the cats was...rough. I had given it descriptions of how I wanted them to look, but what the model came up with was alien, very much the representation of a machine's best approximation of what humans and animals look like. There were other problems with the first iteration in terms of timing, explosions not being big enough, but the appearance was the main problem.

To fix this, I tried giving the AI more references for what I wanted the visual aesthetic to be, including the Exploding Kittens board game cover art, and other pixel games like Risk of Rain and Noita. These just seemed to make things worse. I eventually got the cats to an acceptable look, but my mom still didn't look anything like herself, and the whole game was sort of centered on her likeness.

I didn't feel comfortable giving Claude a picture of my mom, so what I ended up doing was editing a photo of her in Photoshop and pixelating it to an extent that the basics of her face were still there but the details were gone. This did the trick!

For future games I think the safer bet for sprites is just to purchase sheets rather than using AI to build them.

In any case, the game made my mom laugh and I got my other siblings to play it as well.

## Final Thoughts

Reflecting on these little game experiments is validating in that it makes me realize that making these has taught me something. Even if the lessons are basic like keeping things simple, playing with different game mechanics to alter difficulty, and knowing how to get the AI to produce character sprites with fidelity, it's still more meaningful than just messing around which is kind of how it felt and how I thought of it before writing this.
